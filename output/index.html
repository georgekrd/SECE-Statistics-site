def trend_growth(series: pd.Series) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
    """Annual growth implied by a least-squares fit of ln(count) on year, with its 95% confidence
    interval (t distribution with n - 2 degrees of freedom on the slope's standard error)."""
    x = series.index.to_numpy(dtype=float)
    coef, cov = np.polyfit(x, np.log(series.to_numpy(dtype=float)), 1, cov=True)
    half = stats.t.ppf(0.975, len(x) - 2) * np.sqrt(cov[0, 0])
    return tuple(float(np.expm1(v)) for v in (coef[0], coef[0] - half, coef[0] + half))

Key findings

  • Output reached a record 297 publications in 2025, and 70.4% of all records date from 2010 or later.

  • 2021–2025 produced 1,283 publications, 16.1% more than 2016–2020. On a log-linear trend, output has grown by 3.2% a year since 2015 (95% CI 1.0–5.4%, an interval that excludes zero growth), and by 4.1% a year between the end points 2015 and 2025.

  • Conference papers rose from 16.4% of output in 1996–2000 to 51.2% in 2011–2015; journals have since regained ground, from 47.9% of output in 2011–2015 to 56.3% in 2021–2025. Year by year, though, the journal share has eased from 64.4% in 2020, when conference papers dipped during the COVID-19 pandemic, to 55.9% in 2025.

  • Teams have grown: a publication had 3.2 authors on average in 2001–2005 and 5.2 in 2021–2025, and the share with six or more authors rose from 9.0% to 34.5%.

  • Weighted by the share of SECE authors on each publication, output grew by 7.7% from 2016–2020 to 2021–2025, against 16.1% in full counts.

Publications per year¶

As Figure 1 shows, annual output grew from at most 8 publications a year in the 1980s to 72 in 2000 and a record 297 in 2025, followed by 2024 (290) and 2023 (252). The five-year average reached 256.6 publications a year in 2021–2025, and 70.4% of all records date from 2010 or later. Early counts are small (no records are dated 1982, and no year before 1995 has more than 29) and should be read with care, as Scopus covers older literature less completely.

Growth¶

Measured between end points (Table 2), output grew by 5.8% a year from 2000 to 2025, 4.0% from 2010 and 4.1% from 2015. End-point rates depend on the two years chosen: 2025 is the record year, so the trend fits, which use every year, give lower rates for the recent spans (3.8% a year since 2010, 95% CI 2.8–4.9%, and 3.2% since 2015, 1.0–5.4%). Eleven years are few for a trend: the 2015–2025 interval is wide but still excludes zero growth.

Compared with the previous five years, output rose by 69.5% in 2006–2010; growth then slowed to 8.3% in 2011–2015 and picked up again in 2016–2020 (+25.7%). The latest five years, 2021–2025, produced 1,283 publications against 1,105 in 2016–2020 (+16.1%).

Journals, conferences and books¶

Is output shifting toward journals? Compared with 2011–2015, yes (Figure 4). The journal share fell from 83.6% in 1996–2000 to a low of 47.9% in 2011–2015 and recovered to 56.3% in 2021–2025. Year by year, however, it has eased from 64.4% in 2020 to 55.9% in 2025 as conference output recovered: conference papers fell from 93 in 2019 to 75 in 2020 and 76 in 2021, coinciding with the COVID-19 pandemic, when many conferences were cancelled or moved online, and reached a record 132 in 2024. Books and book chapters remain marginal (67 records, 1.3% of all).

Team size¶

Teams have grown steadily (Figure 5). The mean number of authors per publication rose from 3.2 in 2001–2005 to 5.2 in 2021–2025, and the median from 3 to 5 (in 2025: 5). The share of publications with six or more authors went from 9.0% to 34.5%, while single-author publications fell from 4.2% to 1.4%. The growth is not a side effect of the changing document mix: the mean team size rose both in journal publications (3.2 to 5.3) and in conference papers (3.3 to 5.1).

Full and fractional counts¶

Full counting credits the School with a whole publication however many outside co-authors it has. Weighting each publication by the share of its authors who give an SECE affiliation yields 714 publication equivalents in 2021–2025, 7.7% more than in 2016–2020 (663), against 16.1% in full counts (Figure 6). Between 2006–2010 and 2021–2025, full counts rose by 58.0% and fractional counts by 19.0%. The average SECE share of a publication’s authors fell from 71.1% in 2001–2005 to 55.7% in 2021–2025. In single-institution publications it stayed between 94.3% and 95.9% in every five-year period from 1991–1995 on, so the decline reflects the growing weight of publications with external co-authors (see Collaboration) rather than a drift in how SECE authors are identified.

Notes & caveats¶

  • Coverage. Counts are the records kept from the results of the Scopus affiliation query (see Methodology & data). 5,224 of the 5,226 records carry an SECE affiliation string; in 5 of them it names another AUTH unit (for example the Department of Physics), so 5,219 are confirmed School records. All are counted. Work that Scopus does not index (many national conferences, theses, patents, technical reports) is not counted.

  • Partial year. The export is from 2 November 2026. Of the 246 records dated 2026, 189 are articles and only 42 are conference papers (against 126 in 2025): conference proceedings usually reach Scopus months after the event. 2026 is therefore counted in the totals and in shares of all records, but left out of trends, growth rates, averages and shares by year or period.

  • Articles in press. 26 records are articles in press (25 dated 2026 and 1 dated 2024); their final publication year may still change.

  • Language. 99.9% of the records are in English; the others are in German (2) and Chinese (1).

  • Document types are those assigned by Scopus. The source type (journal, conference, book) is derived from the document type, not from the venue, so a conference paper published in a journal issue counts as conference output.

  • Authors are the author positions listed in Scopus. SECE authors are identified from their affiliation text, so the fractional counts depend on how authors report their affiliation (see Research community).

  • Growth rates are descriptive and sensitive to the years compared: a CAGR uses only its two end years, a trend fit uses every year of the span.
